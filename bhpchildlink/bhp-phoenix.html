An Outlier Among Outliers

Phoenix A is possibly the most peculiar black hole currently known, estimated to be between 80-100 billion M☉ by some astronomers. While it is currently theorized that many black holes gain mass larger than a few thousand masses by black hole mergers and similar mechanisms, so far it remains confusing as to how intermediate or supermassive black holes form. A leading theory is that such massive black holes formed during the early universe, or by quasi-stars formation. These theories don't explain anything larger than 40 billion M☉, and Phoenix A remains conspiciously larger than originally thought possible. Such a thing possibly hints to a mechanism in black hole formation other than quasi-star formation or standard black hole merging.

To explain why 40 billion M☉ black holes seem to be the cut-off is that after a certain point the accretion disk becomes so energetic and the surrounding galaxy so hot as to prevent star formation galaxy wide. This occurs because when the dust that serves as a star precursor is at a higher temperature they bounce off other dust particles instead of gathering together. This in turn hampers star formation, and prevents extremely large black holes from coming into being, such a intermediate and supermassive black holes. These black holes must necessarilly merge in order to form a black hole as gargantuan as Phoenix A, except said process isn't so fast as to produce Phoenix A as it exists. Mass would still fall into such a black hole via the accretion disk, but it would be comparatively slow next to stellar consumption and black hole merging.

The Largest?

For now, it is considered the largest known black hole, although some scientists still debate its real size. What we do know about it is that it's one of the largest black holes known, maybe at the tail end of 40 billion M☉, and possibly even bigger. At the distance it is at, around 5.5 to 8 billion light-years from us, it is extremely difficult to gather substantive information about anything. Many like to ecstaticly exclame that something is the newest great, and that is a consideration when looking at black holes. The biggest is most definitely one we haven't discovered yet, but it may not be appreciably larger than either TON 618 or Phoenix A. What is known about Phoenix A is nonetheless fascinating, as it may periodically shift between a quasar phase and a radiojet phase. This isn't entirely unique to it, but is a very interesting topic of research due to the density of Phoenix cluster which Phoenix A sits in the center of.
