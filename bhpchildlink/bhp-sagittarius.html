Sagittarius A, Importance Beyond Measure

To humans, there are few black holes that are important to us for our survival. In fact, effectively no stellar objects other than the sun are of a high importance to us. The single exception is Sagittarius A. At a distance of very nearly 27,000 light-years, it might be hard to grasp why it's so important. The simple and lazy answer is that Sagittarius A is the galactic nucleus of the Milkyway galaxy, a very active one. Why that is important is the complicated part. Our current understanding of galaxies is that gravity is keeping everything bound more tightly together than it otherwise might be. Whether this be some fantastical "dark matter", micro-black holes, hard to see interstellar dust, or some combination of them is currently unclear. What does remain clear however is the fact that every galaxy known has a galactic nucleus, whether that comes in the form of a difficult to detect and quiet giant, or an active and incredibly hungry nucleus like Sagittarius A is mostly up to chance. Most galaxies have one, which says something about the formation of galaxies themselves in that their formation may be linked to the mass of said supermassive black holes.

In short, what I'm saying is that humans may owe our existence to Sagittarius A. Life seems to be so rare after all, and all the filters that seem to keep other life-forms from existing haven't kept us from existing... or we haven't passed the most dangerous of these filters yet perhaps. Beyond likely owing our existence to Sagittarius A, it is also a universal marvel like all other supermassive black holes, and is likely one of our best options for black hole study.

A Galactic Beacon

I have been talking a lot about active galactic nuclei, and galactic nuclei, and supermassive black holes. To be clear, Sagittarius A is all of these things, but many galaxies don't have some of these things. An active galactic nucleus is a centrally located supermassive black hole, its activity being determined by whether it produces light energy. How can a black hole produce light energy if it's literally called a black hole? If you've read the relativity page I mentioned Kerr black holes, spinning black holes. As black holes spin, the drag space and time with them in a process called frame dragging. You may also remember the Gaia BH1 simulation in the Dark Stars page, which showed a clear example of centrifugal force. As something gets closer to an object it is orbiting or attached to, the object moves faster. This happens with particles that are a part of an accretion disk, which itself is simply a large collection of stellar dust and matter that is contained within a system. The particles increase in speed as they orbit closer and closer to the black hole, generating more and more light. The energy produced by a black hole being fed is an order of magnitude higher than that of stars, and more efficiently as well. An inactive galactic nucleus is simply a supermassive black hole that is lacking such an accretion disk. Sagittarius A still has its accretion disk, and thus its status as an active nucleus. It will continue to feed itself for millenia to come.

A Quasi-Stellar Ghost

Quasi-Stellar Radio Sources, also known as quasars, are extremely distant objects that appear to be moving extremely fast. Fast enough that it's difficult to explain their speed other than the expaansion of the universe. By virtue of light taking a long time to reach us, we are seeing something that does not exist anymore in all likelihood. What does this mean and how is it relavant? Specifically quasars are likely to be a galactic phenomenon from any earlier age, evidenced by the fact that there are very very away, their light taking an extreme and almost incomprehensible amount of time to reach us. In fact, not a single quasar has been detected in the local group, a fact that should not be understated in terms of importance. In this way we may see that Sagittarius A is a quasi-stellar ghost, not dead but ever changing and moving forward unto its next iteration.
