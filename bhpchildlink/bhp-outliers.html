The Largest Outliers in the Universe

Black holes are both rare and engimatic. They seem to rarely form if the rate of neutron stars is anything to go by, and they are difficult to find and study closely. Most of the black holes we've found are the result of obvious tells such as peculiar star orbits around a seeming void, the light produced by accretion of matter into a black hole, or the intense radio-waves given off by them. Beyond some sub-types of black holes previously mentioned, there also exist binary and trinary black hole pairs. These such pairs if close enough are fated to merge at some point, most black holes larger than 50 M☉ expected to have undergone said mergers before. Black holes below around 150 M☉ are currently where most black holes sit. These black holes are what is possible given the age of the universe; anything bigger must necessarilly be due to a different process or because an unusual amount of mergers happened with an individual black hole. As such, anything bigger than 150-250 M☉ is an outlier. To be clear, despite the often sensationalized nature of the largest black holes, this does not change the fact that most black holes are likely the smaller main-sequence black holes.

In knowing what is normal, we can clearly see what is an outlier in regards to black holes. Merging black holes, such as the black holes evidenced by gravitational waves GW231123 or GW190521, are normal. So is anything smaller, whether they possess an accretion disk, another sibling star, or any other inter-system bodies like planets or moons. Despite what popular culture may have you believe, the orbits of any said bodies do not usually terminate within a black hole; these orbits can be just as stable the orbit of our own solar system.

Awesome and Terrifying Gravity

In the Phoenix A page was discussed quasars, massive radio-sources that are so far out and so powerful that they can seldom be imagined for their size and power. There do exist smaller more common variants, although they are much more difficult to detect due to the divergence in size. These microquasars as they are called produce the same radio signatures as the larger typical quasars, just in a smaller package. One such example is GRO J1655-40 that exists within our own galaxy. It is a system with a main sequence F-Class star indicating that the star of the binary has existed for quite a while, likely in spite of its hungry sibling, and its sibling is a larger black hole that is obviously consuming its sibling. In doing so, the black hole is creating massive radio-jets as is expected of active galactic nuclei. In this way GRO J1655-40 is acting as something that we expect to be typically larger, even though more microquasars likely exist than true quasars. In this way, we can see gravity operates on all material equally no matter the size.
