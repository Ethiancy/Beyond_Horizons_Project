Size Matters

Black holes are many things, but something people don't usually call black holes is tiny. Tiny in terms of space taken up yes, but that is usually with the fact of density in mind. It is physically possible for black holes to be the size of an asteroid, a planet, or even the size of your favorite baseball, car, or other comparitively small every day object. Unlike standard and "normal" black holes, we don't know that micro-black holes exist, but they can theoretically exist. These sorts of black holes could be traced back to the early universe, although whether they exist now or ever have existed is of some academic debate. What is shown through some of Hawking's calculations is that black holes of a sufficiently small size don't last for very long due to the radiating of their mass away. If you've heard of the universe "bubbling" with particles popping into existence then annihilating with their pair particle, the same thing occurs at the boundary of a black hole. This is Hawking Radiation, and it occurs with every black hole all the time. Imagine micro black holes like a mouse short lived and radiating away its body heat to such an extent as to shorten its life-span, whereas an elephant dedicates much more of its energy to staying staying cool; a similar thing occurs in respect to black holes. In keeping with the analogy, the surface area of the mouse is much smaller as compared to its surface area, but elephants have a much larger internal volume compared to their surface area so the inverse is true for them. It is entirely possible that black holes have an upper limit as to how many of said cosmic bubbles can be absorbed at once, an effective "surface area" could be imagined although not exactly accurate.

Black holes also come in super sized variants as well, so large and so expansive that properly visualizing their scale becomes difficult. Such a black hole, that up until recently was the biggest we've ever found, is TON 618 and is on the larger end of supermassive black holes and close to being an ultramassive black hole and even categorized as such by some scientists and academics. To be clear, ultramassive black holes are not a proper categorization, but as we discover more larger variants of black holes, I'm sure such a category will be required. The matter being consumed by TON 618 is so immense that it not only outshines its own galaxies, but other galaxies around it, rendering attempted observation of them a fools errand.

The Norm

What I haven't mentioned yet is what is considered the norm; stellar mass black holes, or stellar black holes. These black holes, usually only a few masses to several hundred times the mass of our sun are the most numerous types of black holes. While supermassive and ultramassive black holes are talked about and investigated astronomically, they are not the most accessable or abundant option for direct observation. Rather, a better case could be made for Unicorn or Gaia BH1 due to their relatively short distance from us. Odds are however there is a much closer option to more directly observe that we just can't see or haven't really looked at yet. While is is unclear how black holes larger than a few hundred M☉ come into being and is for now little more than theories, we have a good grasp on how black holes for. That is, supernova or kilonova explosions. When a supernova occurs, a stars hydrogen and helium supply is exhausted and their fission into heavier and heavier elements speeds up, eventually causing the internal balance of energy against gravity to falter, allowing gravity winning out. In the case when a star is not massive enough to collapse completely into a black hole, but not so lacking in mass so as to collapse into a white dwarf. A kilonova explosion however occurs when two neutron stars have an orbit that degenerates until they crash together, creating an even grander and important explosion in that of a kilonova explosion. In the end of a kilonova explosion, black holes are always left, remnants of stars past turned into the most dense and hungry type of stellar object known to us at this point.
