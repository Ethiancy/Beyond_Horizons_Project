Size Matters

Black holes are many things, but something people don't usually call black holes is tiny. Tiny in terms of space taken up yes, but that is usually with the fact of density in mind. It is physically possible for black holes to be the size of an asteroid, a planet, or even the size of your favorite baseball, car, or other comparitively small every day object. Unlike standard and "normal" black holes, we don't know that micro-black holes exist, but they can theoretically exist. These sorts of black holes could be traced back to the early universe, although whether they exist now or ever have existed is of some academic debate. What is shown through some of Hawking's calculations is that black holes of a sufficiently small size don't last for very long due to the radiating of their mass away. If you've heard of the universe "bubbling" with particles popping into existence then annihilating with their pair particle, the same thing occurs at the boundary of a black hole. This is Hawking Radiation, and it occurs with every black hole all the time. Imagine micro black holes like a mouse short lived and radiating away its body heat to such an extent as to shorten its life-span, whereas an elephant dedicates much more of its energy to staying staying cool; a similar thing occurs in respect to black holes. In keeping with the analogy, the surface area of the mouse is much smaller as compared to its surface area, but elephants have a much larger internal volume compared to their surface area so the inverse is true for them. It is entirely possible that black holes have an upper limit as to how many of said cosmic bubbles can be absorbed at once, an effective "surface area" could be imagined although not exactly accurate.

Black holes also come in super sized variants as well, so large and so expansive that properly visualizing their scale becomes difficult. Such a black hole, that up until recently was the biggest we've ever found, is TON 618 and is on the larger end of supermassive black holes and close to being an ultramassive black hole and even categorized as such by some scientists and academics. To be clear, ultramassive black holes are not a proper categorization, but as we discover more larger variants of black holes, I'm sure such a category will be required. The matter being consumed by TON 618 is so immense that it not only outshines its own galaxy, but other galaxies around it, rendering attempted observation of them a fools errand. An interesting fact about TON 618 is that we knew about it for years before it was discovered as a radio-loud quasar by various radio surveys, its gross incandescence being confused for a more local star in the Milkyway galaxy.

The Norm

What I haven't mentioned yet is what is considered the norm; stellar mass black holes, or stellar black holes. These black holes, usually only a few masses to several hundred times the mass of our sun are the most numerous types of black holes. While supermassive and ultramassive black holes are talked about and investigated astronomically, they are not the most accessable or abundant option for direct observation. Rather, a better case could be made for Unicorn or Gaia BH1 due to their relatively short distance from us. Odds are however there is a much closer option to more directly observe that we just can't see or haven't really looked at yet. While is is unclear how black holes larger than a few thousand M☉ come into being and is for now little more than theories, we have a good grasp on how black holes form. That is, supernova or kilonova explosions. When a supernova occurs, a stars hydrogen and helium supply is exhausted and their fission into heavier and heavier elements speeds up, eventually causing the internal balance of energy against gravity to falter, allowing gravity to win out. In the case when a star is not massive enough to collapse completely into a black hole, but not so lacking in mass so as to collapse into a white dwarf. A kilonova explosion however occurs when two neutron stars have an orbit that degenerates until they crash together, creating an even grander and important explosion in that of a kilonova explosion. In the end of a kilonova explosion, black holes are always left, remnants of stars past turned into the most dense and hungry type of stellar object known to us at this point.

Black Hole Types

There aren't many ways to categorize black holes other than by their size. The smallest are stellar black holes, that probably start out at about double the size of our sun. These black holes are almost assuredly the most numerous type. If during a supernova a black hole does not form, then it will be either a white dwarf or a neutron star. As a side-note, neutron stars are similarly rare as black holes, so the chance of our own star becoming anything other than a white dwarf is likely very low. Continuing on, another type are intermediate black holes which are currently something of an enigma to scientists and astronomers everywhere. To be clear, they are extremely rare, no currently confirmed black holes existing past 150 M☉. This brings into question how supermassive and ultramassive black holes came into being. For now, the theories will be left for the theory section of the site. Anyways, supermassive black holes are currently the second most numerous type, and are likely at the center of every galaxy. They can be active, spewing forth both incredibly intense radio-signatures and light, or occasionally just one of the two, or inactive quiet giants subtly influencing the movement of everything orbit around them. Finally are Ultramassive black holes, likely the rarest and scariest sort of celestial body to exist. They can be so bright so as to outshine entire galaxies, and loud enough to prevent radio-observation past them. The radiojets of an ultramassive black hole could sterilize entire systems with their ionizing radiation, and their radiolobes could span tens of galaxies wide.

Accretion disks have already been touched on as well as non-active binary pairs such as Gaia BH1, but what hasn't been mentioned are microquasars, or active binary pairs. These occur when a black hole and its stellar pair are close enough so as to allow the black hole to strip matter from the orbit of its sibling. In this act of celestial cannabalization the small stellar sized black hole creates a small accretion disk and a low-intensity radiojet much like that of a conventional quasar.
