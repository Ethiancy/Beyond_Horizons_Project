Black Hole History

The history of black holes started out before they were even a concept, some physics foundational to them coming into being as a theory. Today of course they are much more than theory, they are a proven part of our existence, affecting and bullying stars that take up much more space albeit not infinitely dense as they are. The first significant contribution to them was Ole Rømer showing that light had a finite speed in 1776, although he never formally published the work, and his methodology was difficult to discern at the time. Evidently, it became widely known enough for him to be credited with being the first to discover that light has a finite speed. As semi-unrelated side-note, Rømer had an assistant name Jean Picard, though this Picard didn't pilot a Federation starship.

Continuing on, just a few years later, Newton proved the inverse-square law and later published his findings at the urging of some of his academic peers in 1684. John Michell in 1784 and Pierre Laplace in 1795 both discuss theoretical bodies with escape velocities faster than light, Michell coining the term "Dark Star". Based on all the previously mentioned evidence, one can see how black holes first got their start. Then came the publication of the General Theory of Relativity by Einstein in 1915, and the discourse surrounding the universe and celestial bodies came to the forefront of academic talk. Just a year later, Karl Schwarzchild calculated a solution to a part of General Relativity in that of the Schwarzchild solution. More than four decades later in 1958, David Finkelstein theorized that the Schwarzchild radius was a causality barrier, something that once passed didn't allow any sort of causal change in terms of the end event of said object; an event horizon. By late 1963, the first known utterance of black hole was made in relation to a gravitational singularity, and before that black holes were known by several names like dark stars and gravitational singularities.

Where We're At

The things we know now about black holes is surprising, considering humans have never directly interacted with one. We know that they slowly radiate away due to Hawking Radiation, we know that light bends around them, we know that something that is thrown in will redshift into complete oblivion once they are past the event horizon.

White Holes, Wormholes, and Astronomic Fantasy

As it relates to fantastical and completely theoretical celestial bodies such as white holes, strange stars, and the existence of wormholes, nothing more can be said than that such things are theory. Perhaps one or more of these things may exist, and they are of similar theoretical make of early black holes in the form of dark stars. Unlike black holes however, there is no direct evidence for either white holes, or wormholes. As fun as they are to think about, the simplest solution is usually the correct solution, and the evidence currently points towards such things not existing.
